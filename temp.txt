Licensing Requirements (U.S.)

Yes — you absolutely need proper licensing. Specifically:

Series 79 (Investment Banking Representative): Required for advising on and structuring equity offerings including ATMs. This is the primary license for ECM bankers.
Series 63: Required in most states for transacting securities business.
Series 7 (General Securities Representative): Required if you are also executing trades — i.e., acting as the sales agent selling shares into the market. Many ATM sales agents have Series 7-licensed traders doing the actual execution.
FINRA membership: The broker-dealer firm itself must be a FINRA member to act as a sales agent in an ATM. You can't sign an equity distribution agreement with an issuer without being a registered broker-dealer.

If you're operating outside the U.S. (e.g., acting as agent for a company listed on a U.S. exchange from another jurisdiction), you'd still need to partner with or be a FINRA-registered broker-dealer to legally execute ATM sales in U.S. markets. Execution Workload — How Much Work Per Deal?

For the broker-dealer, REIT ATMs are relatively low-touch on a day-to-day basis compared to an IPO or follow-on, but they require ongoing operational discipline:

Setup (one-time, heavy): Negotiating the sales agreement, coordinating legal opinions, comfort letters, due diligence bringdowns — similar to a traditional offering but done once for the whole program.
Ongoing (lighter but continuous): The issuer sends periodic placement notices instructing the agent to sell a certain number of shares within price/volume parameters. The agent then executes.
Post-earnings windows: Some ATM sales agreements allow sales in the window between an earnings announcement and the filing of the 10-Q/10-K, but only after an interim diligence "bringdown" is completed — sometimes just a CFO call, sometimes formal written certifications and an 8-K filing. 
Goodwin
Blackout compliance: ATM sales agreements include express prohibitions on placing orders when the issuer possesses material non-public information (MNPI). Some agreements go further and restrict sales during any period when insiders couldn't sell under the company's own trading policy. 
Goodwin
When Do Equity Traders Get Involved?

Traders are involved at the execution stage — after the banker has set up the program and the issuer sends a placement notice. Specifically:

Daily/algorithmic selling: Traders manage the VWAP or TWAP execution to drip shares into the market without moving the price, staying within the 5–10% ADTV guardrail.
Block trade moments: When the issuer wants to sell a larger chunk quickly — perhaps after a strong earnings day — traders shift from passive algo execution to active negotiated block placement with institutional buyers. This is when the trading desk becomes the primary point of contact.
Price/volume monitoring: Traders watch the stock in real time and may pause or accelerate selling based on unusual volume, news flow, or price dislocation.
In short, bankers own the relationship and setup; traders own the day-to-day execution. On a quiet week, a trader might spend an hour managing a REIT ATM. On a post-earnings day with a big placement notice, it can dominate the afternoon.

ATM (At-The-Market) Equity Offerings — Acting as a Financial Agent
Here's how the business works and how agents get paid:

What an ATM Agent Does
In an ATM offering, a company sells shares gradually into the open market at prevailing prices. The agent (typically a broker-dealer) acts as intermediary between the issuing company and the market — executing sales on behalf of the issuer over time.

How MicroStrategy-Style ATM Programs Work
Companies like MicroStrategy file a prospectus supplement with the SEC naming specific broker-dealers as their ATM agents. The agent then:

Receives instructions from the issuer on how many shares to sell and at what minimum price
Sells shares into the market on the issuer's behalf (as agent, not principal)
Remits proceeds minus their commission
How Agents Get Paid
Commission structure:

Typically 1–3% of gross proceeds per transaction
MicroStrategy's ATM filings, for example, specify up to 2% commission to their agents (Cowen, BTIG, etc.)
Some deals include a small fixed fee plus a percentage
Payment flow:

Shares sold → proceeds go to agent's account → agent deducts commission → remits net proceeds to issuer

How to Get Into This Business
To legally act as an ATM agent you need:

Requirement	Detail
FINRA registration	Must be a registered broker-dealer (BD) in the US
Series 79 / Series 7	Key licenses for investment banking and equity transactions
SEC registration	As a BD under the Securities Exchange Act
State registrations	Blue sky laws in states where you operate
Capital requirements	Net capital rules (SEC Rule 15c3-1) — minimum $250K+ for most BDs
Realistic Path to Entry
If you want to be the agent directly:

Register a broker-dealer (very expensive, 12–18 months, $500K–$2M+ in costs)
OR join an existing BD as a registered rep and do deals under their umbrella
If you want to earn referral/finder fees:

Some BDs pay finders for bringing issuers to them
Finders cannot receive transaction-based compensation without BD registration (SEC rules) — this is a legal gray area; get legal counsel
You can structure as a consulting/advisory fee in some cases
More practical entry point:

Work at an existing boutique investment bank or placement agent
Build deal flow relationships with small/mid-cap public companies
Partner with a licensed BD who does the execution while you source deals

Series 79 vs. Series 7 for ATM Agency Work
Short Answer
Series 79 is the more appropriate license for ATM work specifically. Series 7 alone is generally not sufficient for acting as an agent in capital markets transactions like ATMs.

The Key Distinction
License	Scope	Issued By
Series 79	Investment Banking Representative — M&A, equity offerings, debt offerings, ATMs	FINRA
Series 7	General Securities Representative — trading, retail brokerage, secondary market sales	FINRA
Why Series 79 for ATMs
ATM offerings are capital markets / investment banking activities, not retail brokerage. The Series 79 was created specifically to cover:

Equity underwriting and offerings (IPOs, follow-ons, ATMs)
Debt offerings
M&A advisory
Private placements
FINRA designed Series 79 to replace the need for Series 7 in pure investment banking roles. If your firm's business is only capital markets work (not retail trading), Series 79 covers you.

When Series 7 Still Matters
If you also want to execute secondary market trades for clients
If your BD requires it as a base license
Some smaller BDs still use Series 7 as a catch-all
Some firms require both — Series 79 for the IB work and Series 7 for broader trading authority.

Also Required (Often Overlooked)
Series 63 or 66 — state securities law exam (almost always required alongside either license)
SIE (Securities Industry Essentials) — co-requisite for both Series 79 and Series 7 since 2018
Bottom Line
For ATM agency business specifically → Series 79 + SIE + Series 63 is the standard combination. Series 7 alone would leave you outside your licensed scope for underwriting/offering activities.

How does ATM price decide?

How ATM Offering Price Is Determined
Core Principle: The Market Decides in Real Time
Unlike IPOs or traditional follow-on offerings where a fixed price is set in advance, ATM offerings have no pre-set price. Shares are sold directly into the open market at whatever the current trading price is at the moment of sale.

The Mechanism
Issuer (e.g. Strategy) tells the agent how many shares to sell and sets a floor price (minimum acceptable price)
Agent executes sales into the open market like normal market orders/trades
Each share sells at the prevailing market price at that moment
Different shares in the same day's ATM sale can sell at different prices
So the "ATM price" is essentially a volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of all executions during the selling period.

Who Controls What
Parameter	Controlled By
Minimum floor price	Issuer
Timing of sales	Issuer (instructs agent daily)
How to execute (pace, timing within day)	Agent
Actual execution price	The open market
Maximum shares to sell per day	Issuer
Why This Matters vs. Traditional Offerings
ATM	Traditional Follow-On
Price set by	Live market	Overnight book-building
Discount to market	Minimal (0–1%)	Typically 4–7% discount
Flexibility	High	Low
Speed	Continuous	One-time event
MSTR Specific Example
Strategy sells into the market when MSTR is trading actively. Because MSTR trades millions of shares daily, the agent can sell hundreds of thousands of shares without meaningfully moving the price. The sheer liquidity of MSTR is what makes their ATM so powerful — most companies can't absorb that volume without crashing their own stock price.

Key Risk: Dilution Pressure
If the market notices heavy ATM selling, the stock price can drift lower — which is why agents are instructed to sell gradually and discreetly, blending into normal trading volume so as not to signal to the market that a large sell program is underway.

ATM Discount — The Reality
Short Answer: Technically no fixed discount, but there are implicit advantages that attract buyers
You're right that if the ATM price equals the market price, why would anyone buy new shares from the offering instead of just buying on the open market? Here's how it actually works:

The ATM Buyer Is NOT a Retail Investor
This is the crucial misunderstanding. ATM shares are NOT sold to specific investors at a negotiated price. The agent sells shares into the anonymous open market — buyers don't even know they're buying newly issued shares vs. secondary shares.

Think of it this way:

Strategy tells Morgan Stanley "sell 500,000 shares today"
Morgan Stanley executes those as market sell orders throughout the day
Buyers on the other side just see normal trades on Nasdaq
They have no idea if shares came from existing holders or new issuance
So Where Does the "Discount" Concept Come In?
Situation	Discount?
Pure ATM into open market	No explicit discount
Block trades within ATM program	Small negotiated discount (1–3%)
Compared to traditional follow-on	ATM is actually better for the issuer — no 5–7% discount required
Why the Market Still Absorbs It
ATM works because of natural market demand — there are always buyers on the other side of every trade. The agent just adds supply gradually enough that it doesn't overwhelm demand and crash the price.

For MSTR specifically:

Daily volume often exceeds 10–20 million shares
ATM sales might be 500K–2M shares per day
That's a small fraction of normal volume — easily absorbed invisibly
The Exception: Bought Deals & Block Trades
When Strategy needs to sell a large chunk quickly, they do a negotiated block trade — here a real discount of 2–5% is offered to institutional buyers to take a large block at once. This is sometimes done under the ATM agreement as an alternative execution method.

Bottom Line
The ATM model is elegant precisely because it avoids the discount problem — by dripping shares into existing market volume, the issuer captures the full market price while buyers simply participate in normal trading without realizing they're part of a capital raise.
